AP CSP Multiple Choice: Question Types, Weightings and How to Practise Them

By Syed Shahmikh Ali · Published 2026-11-10 · Updated 2026-11-10

The AP Computer Science Principles multiple-choice section is 70 questions in 2 hours and counts for 70% of your score, so it matters more than the Create task. Most of the questions are single-select, a small group use a reading passage, and 8 ask you to pick two answers. The best way to prepare is to match your practice to the topic weights, because almost a third of the section is Algorithms and Programming.

Key takeaways

  • Section I has 70 questions in 120 minutes with 4 answer options each: 57 single-select, 5 single-select tied to a reading passage, and 8 multiple-select where you choose 2 answers.
  • The section is 70% of the exam score. The Create task and written responses make up the other 30%.
  • Algorithms and Programming is 30% to 35% of the multiple-choice section, and Impact of Computing is 21% to 26%, so those two big ideas cover over half of it.
  • The College Board does not publish past multiple-choice questions, so you need a teacher with AP Classroom access or your own practice questions.
  • Time yourself: 120 minutes for 70 questions works out to under two minutes each, which leaves little room for slow code tracing.

What is the AP CSP multiple-choice section like?

It is 70 questions in 120 minutes, worth 70% of your score, with four answer options per question. The AP Computer Science Principles exam page lists the split: 57 single-select questions, 5 single-select questions tied to a reading passage about a computing innovation, and 8 multiple-select questions where you select 2 answers. The page also gives the 2027 exam date as 14 May, and says the exam is fully digital in the Bluebook app.

The College Board's exam instructions say your multiple-choice score is based solely on the number of questions you answer correctly, and the page does not mention a penalty for wrong answers. In the instructions I read, calculators are not allowed. That means an unanswered question can never beat a guess.

Which topics carry the most weight?

Algorithms and Programming carries the most weight, at 30% to 35% of the multiple-choice section. The course and exam description gives the exam weighting for each of the five big ideas:

Big ideaShare of the multiple-choice section
1. Creative Development10% to 13%
2. Data17% to 22%
3. Algorithms and Programming30% to 35%
4. Computer Systems and Networks11% to 15%
5. Impact of Computing21% to 26%

Big ideas 3 and 5 together are 51% to 61% of the section. A student who spends equal time on all five is under-preparing for the two that count the most. I would put roughly half of your practice into those two, and split the rest across the other three.

How are code questions asked?

They ask you to work out what a piece of code does. The course description says that on the exam, students "will be asked to determine the result or functionality of code," and the exam reference sheet gives the programming constructs in both text and block forms, including operators such as a MOD b.

The skill is tracing, and it is worth practising on paper. Here is a small example I wrote in the style of the exam pseudocode. It is my own example, not a College Board question:

total ← 0
FOR EACH n IN [4, 7, 2, 9]
{
    IF (n MOD 2 = 0)
    {
        total ← total + n
    }
}
DISPLAY(total)

What is displayed? Keep a small table with one column for n, one for whether n MOD 2 is 0, and one for total. The value 4 is even, so total becomes 4. The value 7 is odd, so nothing changes. The value 2 is even, so total becomes 6. The value 9 is odd. The program displays 6.

The habit to build is writing the table every time, even when the code looks easy. Questions of this type go wrong when students do the arithmetic in their head and skip a row.

What are the reading passage questions?

They are 5 single-select questions that come with a passage about a computing innovation. The course description's note for Big Idea 5 says students are presented with a passage about a computing innovation and asked questions about its data and effects.

Read the questions before the passage. The questions tell you what to look for, so you read once instead of twice, and you can skip detail that no question uses. Check that the answer you choose is supported by what the passage says and not by what you already know about the technology. A familiar example from the news can pull you towards an answer the passage does not back.

How should you handle multiple-select questions?

Treat them as two separate decisions. The exam instructions tell students to mark the two best answer choices for these questions. The sources I read do not explain how a partially correct pair is scored, so do not assume partial credit and do not guess a strategy around it.

What does work is judging each option as true or false on its own, then choosing the two that are clearly true. With four options and two correct ones, if you can rule out one wrong option with confidence, the remaining choices are much easier. Practise this in the same session as your single-select questions so the format does not feel new on exam day.

How can you practise if past papers are not public?

Use your teacher's AP Classroom access, and write your own questions for the rest. The College Board's past exam questions page lists free-response questions and scoring materials by year, but no multiple-choice questions. It says that educators authorized to teach the course can access practice questions in AP Classroom.

Here is a weekly routine you can run without a published paper:

  1. Two timed blocks of 20 questions. At 120 minutes for 70 questions, the exam pace is a little under 1 minute 43 seconds per question. Aim for about 34 minutes per block of 20, and note which question types ran over.
  2. Mark every wrong answer with a cause. Use three labels: did not know the idea, misread the question, or made a tracing slip. The label tells you what to fix.
  3. Write one question yourself. Pick a topic from big idea 3 or 5, write a question with four options, and explain why each wrong option is wrong. It is slow, and it teaches more than answering ten questions.
  4. Trace one program on paper, no shortcuts. Use a variable table like the one above.

The Create task has its own preparation. I cover it in how the AP CSP Create written responses score, and the general method for timed practice is in how to use past papers for computer science revision.

What should a six-week plan look like?

Weight your weeks the same way the exam weights its content. This plan is my suggestion, built from the weightings above, and you should adjust it to your own gaps.

  • Weeks 1 and 2: Algorithms and Programming. Tracing, lists, loops, conditionals and procedures, with a variable table for every program.
  • Week 3: Impact of Computing, with reading passage practice.
  • Week 4: Data, then Computer Systems and Networks.
  • Week 5: Creative Development, plus a mixed timed set of 35 questions.
  • Week 6: Two full-length timed attempts if you have the questions, and review of every wrong answer by cause.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a penalty for wrong answers on AP CSP multiple choice?

The exam instructions say the multiple-choice score is based solely on the number of questions answered correctly, and they do not mention a penalty. That means you should answer every question, even if you have to guess.

Can I use a calculator on the AP CSP exam?

The exam instructions I read state that calculators are not allowed for this exam. The reference sheet includes operators such as MOD, so you practise arithmetic by hand.

Where can I find free AP CSP multiple-choice practice questions?

The College Board's past exam questions page lists only free-response materials. Practice multiple-choice questions are available to authorized teachers through AP Classroom, so ask your teacher to assign them, and write your own questions to fill the gaps.
